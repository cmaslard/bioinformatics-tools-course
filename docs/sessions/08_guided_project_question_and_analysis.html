8.1.1 Learning outcomes

By the end of this session, you should be able to:

  • explore an unknown dataset (what is one row, which columns, how many samples per group) before any statistics;
  • turn a broad theme into a focused question, with a hypothesis and the result that would refute it;
  • name the response variable, the explanatory variables and the possible confounders of your question;
  • choose, for each step of the analysis, a tool from Sessions 2-7, and justify it;
  • make a first exploratory figure that already says something about the question;
  • split the work in a group so that everyone understands the whole project.

8.2 Theory (≈30 min)

8.2.1 1. From data to a question

A dataset is not a question. The soybean tables of Sessions 4 and 5 could answer “which bacteria respond to drought?”, “do the two genotypes differ?” or “does the root release other molecules than the leaf contains?”: three different analyses. A good project question is:

  • biological: it is about organisms, not about the data (“which molecules change?” is a step, “does the plant make protective molecules in the root before the leaf?” is a question);
  • answerable with these data: the variables it needs are in the table;
  • focused: one main question, two or three sub-questions at most;
  • refutable: you can say in advance which result would mean “no”.

A useful exercise: write your question, then the figure that would answer it (axes, colours, panels), then the result you expect on that figure. If you cannot draw the figure, the question is not yet precise enough.

8.2.2 2. The anatomy of a plan

Your PLAN.qmd has seven parts. Each is short:

Hypothesis and refutation What you expect, and the result that would prove you wrong More molecules change in the root, which meets the dry soil first; refuted if the leaf has as many or more.
Data Files, rows, the subset you use, how many samples per group One row per sample and molecule; root and leaf only; 2 genotypes × 4 conditions × 3-4 plants

Method, step by step Each step and the course tool that does it 1. log of the concentrations; 2. PCA per organ (Session 5); 3. one lm() per molecule with FDR (Sessions 5-6) …

Risks and roles What could go wrong, and who does what Four plants per group is few; X does the PCA, Y the models …

8.2.3 3. The toolbox: which tool for which question?

If your question is … Tool Session
How many, how big, per group? group_by(), summarise(), count() 4
Is there a difference between groups? Does it depend on a second factor? lm(y ~ a * b), anova(), boxplot, interaction plot 4
What are the main patterns among many variables? Which samples resemble each other? prcomp(), heatmap, clustering 5
Which of thousands of features (genes, SNPs, OTUs) are associated with a variable? one lm() per feature with apply(), Bonferroni or FDR, Manhattan or volcano plot 5, 6
Is an association real, or due to a confounder (ancestry, family, batch)? add the confounder to lm(), or PCs as covariates 6
Can a variable be predicted from the others, and how well on new data? decision tree, random forest, train/test split, confusion matrix 7
Is this sequence similar to a known one? How are these genes related? blastp, mafft, FastTree 3
Where is it? a map with geom_point() or geom_tile() 4, 6

Two rules. First explore, then test: a plot of the raw data comes before any model. One question, several tools: a good project usually chains three or four of them (for example: exploration, PCA, one model per gene, then a closer look at the best genes).

Your workbooks are your code library. Your files of Sessions 3 to 7 (in ~/bioinfo_course/session_03/ to session_07/) hold working code for every tool of this table. Copy a block that works and adapt it to your tables, rather than starting from an empty chunk.

8.2.4 4. The classic traps

You met each of them in the course; they all come back in real data.

  • The confounder (Session 6): an association created by a third variable. Accessions from the north flower late and share many alleles; in a lake survey, the fish of the deep station are larger and older.
  • The batch: samples processed on different days, by different people, in different experiments. Check whether it explains your first PC.
  • Thousands of tests (Sessions 5-6): with 20,000 genes, 1,000 are “significant” at 5% by chance alone.
  • Unequal sampling: more observations does not mean more organisms. Ten times more records of a butterfly in 2020 than in 1980 tells you first about the number of observers.
  • Raw counts are not comparable: a sample sequenced twice as deep has twice as many reads in every gene. Normalise first (counts per million, then a log).
  • Overfitting (Session 7): a model judged on the data it learned from always looks good. Keep a test set.
  • Correlation is not causation: the data are observations; say what an experiment would need to show.

8.3.1 A. How fast does a plant answer a bacterium?

The biology. Plants have no immune cells: every cell defends itself. A first layer of immunity recognises general bacterial molecules. Bacteria inject effector proteins to suppress it, and plants evolved resistance (R) genes whose products recognise some of these effectors and trigger a stronger, faster response, often with cell death at the infection site. The strain Pseudomonas syringae pv. tomato DC3000 is virulent on Arabidopsis: no effector is recognised. The same strain carrying the effector AvrRpt2 (recognised by the R protein RPS2) or AvrRpm1 (recognised by RPM1) is avirulent: the plant wins. Several hormones carry the signal: salicylic acid (SA, needs the gene SID2), and the regulators PAD4, EIN2 (ethylene) and DDE2 (jasmonate).

The data. Leaves of six genotypes were infiltrated with water (mock) or one of the three strains and harvested from 1 to 24 hours later, in three independent trials (Mine et al. 2018).

File One row is Columns
pseudomonas_samples.csv one sample (366) sample, genotype (Col-0 = wild type, sid2, pad4, pad4 sid2, dde2 ein2 pad4 sid2, rpm1 rps2), treatment (mock, DC3000, AvrRpt2, AvrRpm1), time_h, trial, library_size
pseudomonas_counts.csv.gz one gene (18,422) gene_id, then one column per sample: the number of reads
arabidopsis_genes.csv one gene gene_id, symbol, position, description (as in Session 6)

Not every genotype was tested with every strain at every time: count the samples per combination first.

Starting question. Does wild-type Arabidopsis (Col-0) respond faster to a bacterium it recognises (AvrRpt2, AvrRpm1) than to one it does not (DC3000)? How many genes change, and when?

To go further.

  • Which hormone pathway does each mutant lose? Compare the response of the mutants to AvrRpt2 with Col-0, and follow known marker genes (look for PR1 in the gene table).
  • In rpm1 rps2, the plant cannot recognise the effectors any more. Does AvrRpt2 then look like DC3000?
  • Do the response genes fall into a few groups with the same time course (early, transient, late)? What are these genes?
  • Is the trial (the day of the experiment) visible in the data?

Tools. Normalisation (counts per million, log); PCA (Session 5); for each time point, the difference with mock, or one lm() per gene (Session 6) with a correction for thousands of tests; heatmap and clustering (Session 5).

Watch out. Compare each infected sample with the mock of the same time: the leaves change during the day too. A trial is a batch. With 18,000 genes, choose your threshold before you look.

8.3.2 B. What controls the expression of a gene in nature?

The biology. In Session 6 you found the genes behind flowering time and leaf sodium by comparing 1,003 wild accessions. Here, the “trait” is the expression of a gene: the leaves of 649 of the same accessions were sequenced (RNA-seq) before flowering, all grown in the same chamber (Kawakatsu et al. 2016). An expression level can be controlled by a variant next to the gene (in cis) or by a variant in another gene that regulates it (in trans). A GWAS whose trait is an expression level is called an eQTL scan.

The data.

File One row is Columns
accessions.csv one accession (649) as in Session 6 (accession_id, name, country, latitude, longitude, genetic_group, flowering_10C, flowering_16C, leaf_sodium), plus the climate of its place of origin (WorldClim, 1970-2000): annual_mean_temperature, min_temperature_coldest_month, annual_precipitation and five more
accession_expression.csv.gz one gene (20,832) gene_id, then one column per accession (acc_ + accession_id, in the order of accessions.csv): normalised read counts
accession_genotypes.csv.gz one SNP (22,406) as in Session 6: snp_id, chromosome, position, then 0/1 per accession, same order
arabidopsis_genes.csv one gene as in Session 6

Starting question. The gene FLC blocks flowering. Is its expression in the leaves associated with flowering time? And which regions of the genome control the expression of FLC: the region of FLC itself, or other regions?

To go further.

  • Do the accessions group by their expression (PCA) the way they group by their DNA (genetic groups)?
  • Which genes are expressed differently in accessions from cold and warm places? Is it the climate, or the ancestry that goes with it?
  • Choose other genes (another flowering gene, a gene of a pathway you like) and run their eQTL scan: cis, trans or nothing?
  • Can the expression of a few genes predict flowering time better than FLC alone (Session 7, with a test set)?

Tools. Log of the expression; lm() and correlation (Session 4); PCA (Session 5); GWAS with apply(), Manhattan and QQ plots, PCs as covariates (Session 6); trees and test sets (Session 7).

Watch out. Population structure (Session 6) is still there. A scan of 22,000 SNPs for each of 20,000 genes would be 440 million models: choose a few genes. Climate of origin and ancestry are confounded.

8.3.3 C. When does a coral break down under heat?

The biology. A reef-building coral is a partnership: the animal hosts microscopic algae (Symbiodiniaceae) inside its cells, which give it most of its food by photosynthesis. When the sea gets too warm, the partnership breaks down: the coral loses its algae and turns white (bleaching), and may starve. Corals of the northern Red Sea (Gulf of Aqaba) are unusually heat-tolerant. Savary et al. (2021) exposed fragments of five colonies (genotypes) of Stylophora pistillata to 27 °C (normal), 29.5, 32 and 34.5 °C, in a short experiment (a few hours, as in a heatwave) and a long one (about a week), and sequenced both partners at the end of the heat and after recovery.

The data.

File One row is Columns
coral_samples.csv one sample (80) sample, experiment (short, long), time (end_of_heat, recovery), temperature, genotype, coral_reads, algae_reads, algae_share
coral_counts.csv.gz one coral gene (19,722) gene_id, then one column per sample: reads
algae_counts.csv.gz one algal gene (32,605) the same, for the algae
coral_genes.csv.gz, algae_genes.csv.gz one gene gene_id, the best match in protein databases (best_hit_description, best_hit_species, identity_percent), go_terms

Starting question. At which temperature does the coral switch on a large stress response, and does it recover afterwards? Does the answer differ between the short and the long experiment?

To go further.

  • algae_share is the share of reads that come from the algae. Can it measure bleaching? When does it drop?
  • Do the algae respond to heat like their host, at the same temperature?
  • What matters more for expression: the genotype of the colony or the temperature (share of variation, Session 4)?
  • Which kinds of genes change (look at the descriptions: heat shock proteins, antioxidants …)? Are they the same in the short and the long experiment?

Tools. Normalisation; PCA (Session 5); lm() with several factors and share of variation (Session 4); one model per gene with a correction (Sessions 5-6).

Watch out. Five genotypes per group: very few. Genotype is a block, as trial in project A. Gene descriptions come from the best match in other species, often far away (mouse, human): a name is a hint, not a proof.

8.3.4 D. What shapes the communities of ocean bacteria?

The biology. One litre of sea water holds about a billion bacteria and archaea; they make half of the photosynthesis on Earth and recycle most nutrients. Between 2009 and 2013, the schooner Tara sampled the oceans of the world at three depths: the surface (SRF), the deep chlorophyll maximum (DCM, where algae are most abundant, 20-200 m) and the dark mesopelagic zone (MES, 200-1,000 m). Sunagawa et al. (2015) sequenced the DNA of everything filtered from these waters; here you receive the 16S rRNA reads, grouped into taxa (OTUs), as in the soybean microbiome of Session 4.

The data.

File One row is Columns
tara_samples.csv one sample (139) sample, station, date, latitude, longitude, layer (SRF, DCM, MES, MIX), oxygen_minimum_zone, size_fraction, biome, ocean, depth_m, temperature, salinity, oxygen, nitrate, phosphate, silicate, light_par, bacteria_per_ml
tara_otus.csv.gz one OTU (10,311) otu_id, taxonomy (domain to genus), then one column per sample: reads
tara_functions.csv.gz one metabolic pathway (515 KEGG modules) module, then one column per sample: abundance of the genes of that pathway

Starting question. Which factor best explains the composition of the bacterial community: depth (layer), temperature, or the ocean where it was sampled?

To go further.

  • Is diversity (Shannon, Session 4) higher in warm or in cold water, at the surface or in the dark?
  • Which taxa follow temperature? Look at the Cyanobacteria (Prochlorococcus, Synechococcus): what do they need?
  • Do the functions (KEGG modules) vary between samples as much as the taxa? If not, what could that mean?
  • What is special about the oxygen minimum zones?

Tools. Relative abundances, Shannon diversity and lm() (Session 4); PCA of the communities (Session 5, on transformed relative abundances); maps (Sessions 4, 6).

Watch out. Depth, temperature, light and oxygen go together: separating them needs a model with several variables. Two size fractions (filters) exist: do not compare them by accident. Reads per sample differ: work with relative abundances.

8.3.5 E. What does the shape of a bird tell about its life?

The biology. A bird’s body is a toolkit. The beak is shaped by what it eats, the wing by how far it flies, the legs by where it moves. AVONET (Tobias et al. 2022) measured 90,000 museum and live birds of almost every species alive (11,009): beak, wing, tail, legs and mass, with each species’ diet, habitat, migration and geographic range. Classic “rules” of ecology can be tested with it, such as Bergmann’s rule: animals are larger in colder climates.

The data.

File One row is Columns
bird_species.csv one species (11,009) species, family, order, n_individuals, beak (beak_length_culmen, beak_length_nares, beak_width, beak_depth), tarsus_length, wing_length, kipps_distance, secondary1, hand_wing_index (long, pointed wings are high), tail_length, mass, traits_inferred, habitat, habitat_density, migration (1 sedentary, 2 partial, 3 migratory), trophic_level, trophic_niche, primary_lifestyle, latitudes and range_size
bird_individuals.csv.gz one measured bird (90,003) species, sex, specimen_type, country, the same measurements

All lengths are in mm, mass in g, latitudes in degrees.

Starting question. Can you tell what a bird eats from the shape of its body? Which measurements separate the diets best?

To go further.

  • Does a decision tree or a random forest (Session 7) predict the diet on species it has never seen? Which diets does it confuse, and why?
  • Are birds larger closer to the poles (Bergmann’s rule)? Does it still hold within families?
  • Do migratory birds have longer, more pointed wings (hand_wing_index)?
  • Within a species, do males and females differ (bird_individuals.csv.gz)? In which families most?

Tools. Log of the measurements; PCA (Session 5); lm() with a covariate (Sessions 4, 6); trees, random forest, test set and confusion matrix (Session 7).

Watch out. Mass dominates everything: a large bird has a large beak. Think about size before shape (ratios, or the PCA). Species are not independent: related species resemble each other (as related accessions in Session 6); family can enter the model. Some trait values were inferred from related species (traits_inferred).

8.3.6 F. How many immune genes does a plant need?

The biology. The resistance genes of project A belong to one big family: the NLRs (nucleotide-binding, leucine-rich repeat receptors), the main intracellular immune receptors of plants. Every NLR has a central NB-ARC domain. Most also carry an N-terminal domain that defines their class: TIR (TNLs), a coiled coil (CNLs) or RPW8 (RNLs, “helpers” that relay the signal). TNLs need the protein EDS1 (with PAD4, met in project A) to signal. Arabidopsis has about 170 NLR genes, rice 350 to 450, bread wheat more than 2,000, and some plants almost none.

The data. For the reference genome (proteome) of 413 green plants in UniProt, from algae and mosses to trees and grasses, the genes whose proteins carry each domain (domains detected by Pfam).

File One row is Columns
plant_proteomes.csv one species proteome_id, species, lineage_group (green algae, bryophytes …, monocots, eudicots), order, family, n_genes, busco_complete (completeness of the genome annotation, %), nlr_genes (genes with NB-ARC), nlr_genes_with_lrr, tnl_genes, cnl_genes, rnl_genes, eds1_family_genes, lineage
nlr_genes.csv.gz one NLR gene proteome_id, species, lineage_group, gene, accession, length, class (TNL, RNL, CNL or other), lrr, domains (all Pfam domains of the protein)
pfam_domains.csv one Pfam domain the names of the 30 most frequent domains
nlr_proteins.fasta one protein the NLR proteins of eight species, for BLAST, alignments and trees

Starting question. How many NLR genes do plants have? Does this number follow their lineage, the size of their genome, or their way of life?

To go further.

  • Which lineages have no TNL at all? How many genes of the EDS1 family (EDS1, PAD4, SAG101, the partners TNLs need to signal) do they keep, compared with plants that have TNLs? What would that suggest about how the receptors and their partners evolved?
  • Find aquatic, parasitic or carnivorous plants in the table (search their names): how many NLRs do they keep?
  • Do NLRs appear in algae, mosses, ferns? When did the TIR and RPW8 classes appear?
  • Align the NB-ARC proteins of two species and build a tree (Session 3): do the TNLs and the CNLs form separate groups? Do the genes of one species form clusters (recent duplications)?

Tools. Tables, lm() with a covariate (Session 4); blastp, mafft, FastTree and a tree in R (Session 3); a model per lineage (Session 6 logic of confounders).

Watch out. A badly annotated genome “has” fewer genes of every kind: check busco_complete. Larger gene sets have more of everything: compare shares, or put n_genes in the model. Species are not independent: lineage is a confounder. A domain not found by Pfam is not proof that the gene is absent.

8.3.7 G. Will species move with the climate?

The biology. As the climate warms, many species are expected to shift their range towards the poles and up the mountains. Citizen scientists and museums have recorded millions of observations, gathered by GBIF (Global Biodiversity Information Facility). Combined with climate maps (WorldClim), they describe the climatic niche of a species: the climates where it lives. A model of presence from climate can then be projected onto the climate expected in 2061-2080.

The data. Observations of eight species in Europe, two periods (1970-1999 and 2000-2024), on a grid of cells of 10 arc-minutes (about 18 × 12 km in Europe): four warm-loving species expanding northwards (praying mantis Mantis religiosa, scarlet dragonfly Crocothemis erythraea, wasp spider Argiope bruennichi, scarce swallowtail Iphiclides podalirius), two cold-adapted butterflies (Apollo Parnassius apollo, dewy ringlet Erebia pandrose) and two trees (beech Fagus sylvatica, Norway spruce Picea abies).

File One row is Columns
species.csv one species species, common_name, group, and the number of GBIF records per period
occurrences.csv one species × period × cell where it was recorded species, period, longitude, latitude (centre of the cell), n_records
europe_climate.csv.gz one land cell of Europe longitude, latitude, then each climate variable now (1970-2000) and in 2061-2080 under a medium (_ssp245) and a high (_ssp585) emission scenario

Starting question. In which part of the European climate does each species live? Has the range of the warm-loving species moved north between 1970-1999 and 2000-2024?

To go further.

  • Train a model that predicts presence from climate (a tree or a random forest, Session 7: presences against cells where the species was not recorded). Is it good on cells it has not seen?
  • Project it onto the climate of 2061-2080 and map it (geom_tile(), Session 4): which species gain, which lose? Does the scenario matter?
  • Are the cold-adapted species retreating up the mountains or north?
  • Is Norway spruce really living where its “niche” says (think of forestry)?

Tools. Tables and maps with geom_tile() (Session 4); lm(); trees, random forest, test set (Session 7).

Watch out. Sampling effort: there are many more records after 2000, so a species seems to “appear” everywhere. Compare with the number of records, or use the edge of the range (the 95th percentile of latitude) rather than the number of cells. Not recorded does not mean absent. To keep the files small, at most 1,500 records were taken per species, period and country. A species is recorded in a few thousand of the 38,004 cells: a model may simply answer “absent” everywhere and still be right 86 to 98% of the time (the accuracy trap of Session 7); one solution is to take, at random, as many cells without record as cells with a presence. Planted trees live outside their natural niche.

TipHelp for project G: from the past to the future

Three periods, not two. The climate called _now is the average of 1970-2000: it matches your old observations (1970-1999), not today. There is no climate map for 2000-2024, when Europe was already warmer. The future (_ssp245, _ssp585) is 2061-2080. Say which period each figure is about.

What links the periods: the climatic niche. The whole project assumes that a species keeps the same niche and moves to follow it. Your story can go: has it already moved (past to present), and where will its climate be (present to future)?

Past to present: has the range moved?

  • The northern edge of a range is the latitude below which most of the occupied cells lie, for example 95% of them (the 95th percentile of latitude). Why not the northernmost cell? One lost individual or one typing error would move it.
  • More records after 2000 means more cells, everywhere. Before concluding, check that the edge still moves when you give both periods the same number of cells (draw the recent cells at random).
  • The 1970-2000 climate can serve as a fixed reference map: in the recent period, does the species live in cells that used to be too cold for it?

A model of presence (Session 7).

  • One row per cell: present, or not recorded (not “absent”).
  • Balance the two classes before training. What does a model that always says “absent” score?
  • Keep test cells aside; the model never sees them.
  • Use the climate variables only, not longitude and latitude: with the coordinates the tree learns where the species is, not which climate it likes.
  • Which observations do you train on? The old ones match the _now climate in time; the recent ones are more numerous. Choose, and say why.
  • Judge it on the test cells: confusion matrix, accuracy (what does chance give with balanced classes?), recall of the presences. Read the tree: is its first split biologically sensible?

Present to future: projecting the model.

  • The tree knows the climate variables by their _now names. To give it the climate of 2061-2080, make a table where the _ssp245 (or _ssp585) columns carry the _now names. Otherwise predict() cannot find them, or uses the present climate without telling you.
  • Compare, for now, the medium and the high scenario: how many cells are suitable, and where (their median latitude)? Maps side by side: geom_tile(), one facet per period.
  • Does the movement you measured between 1970 and 2024 go the same way as the one the model predicts for 2070?

Limits worth a slide. A suitable climate is not a presence (dispersal, habitat, host plants, history); cells “not recorded” were used as absences; sampling effort changed a lot; the niche may not stay the same; there is no climate map of 2000-2024.

## Theory (≈30 min)

### 1. From data to a question

A dataset is not a question. The soybean tables of Sessions 4 and 5 could answer "which
bacteria respond to drought?", "do the two genotypes differ?" or "does the root release
other molecules than the leaf contains?": three different analyses. A good project
question is:

- **biological**: it is about organisms, not about the data ("which molecules change?"
  is a step, "does the plant make protective molecules in the root before the leaf?" is a
  question);
- **answerable with these data**: the variables it needs are in the table;
- **focused**: one main question, two or three sub-questions at most;
- **refutable**: you can say in advance which result would mean "no".

A useful exercise: write your question, then the figure that would answer it (axes,
colours, panels), then the result you expect on that figure. If you cannot draw the
figure, the question is not yet precise enough.

### 2. The anatomy of a plan

Your `PLAN.qmd` has seven parts. Each is short:

| Part | What to write | Example (the soybean plants of Sessions 4-5) |
|---|---|---|
| Question | One sentence, then two or three sub-questions | Does drought change the chemistry of the root more than that of the leaf? |
| Hypothesis and refutation | What you expect, and the result that would prove you wrong | More molecules change in the root, which meets the dry soil first; refuted if the leaf has as many or more. |
| Data | Files, rows, the subset you use, how many samples per group | One row per sample and molecule; root and leaf only; 2 genotypes × 4 conditions × 3-4 plants |
| Variables | Response, explanatory, possible confounders | Response: concentrations; explanatory: drought, organ; other factors to keep in the model: genotype, heatwave |
| Method, step by step | Each step and the course tool that does it | 1. log of the concentrations; 2. PCA per organ (Session 5); 3. one `lm()` per molecule with FDR (Sessions 5-6) ... |
| First figure | One exploratory figure with a caption, and what it already shows | PC1 vs PC2 of each organ, coloured by condition |
| Risks and roles | What could go wrong, and who does what | Four plants per group is few; X does the PCA, Y the models ... |

### 3. The toolbox: which tool for which question?

| If your question is ... | Tool | Session |
|---|---|---|
| How many, how big, per group? | `group_by()`, `summarise()`, `count()` | 4 |
| Is there a difference between groups? Does it depend on a second factor? | `lm(y ~ a * b)`, `anova()`, boxplot, interaction plot | 4 |
| What are the main patterns among many variables? Which samples resemble each other? | `prcomp()`, heatmap, clustering | 5 |
| Which of thousands of features (genes, SNPs, OTUs) are associated with a variable? | one `lm()` per feature with `apply()`, Bonferroni or FDR, Manhattan or volcano plot | 5, 6 |
| Is an association real, or due to a confounder (ancestry, family, batch)? | add the confounder to `lm()`, or PCs as covariates | 6 |
| Can a variable be **predicted** from the others, and how well on new data? | decision tree, random forest, train/test split, confusion matrix | 7 |
| Is this sequence similar to a known one? How are these genes related? | `blastp`, `mafft`, `FastTree` | 3 |
| Where is it? | a map with `geom_point()` or `geom_tile()` | 4, 6 |

Two rules. **First explore, then test**: a plot of the raw data comes before any model.
**One question, several tools**: a good project usually chains three or four of them (for
example: exploration, PCA, one model per gene, then a closer look at the best genes).

**Your workbooks are your code library.** Your files of Sessions 3 to 7 (in
`~/bioinfo_course/session_03/` to `session_07/`) hold working code for every tool of this
table. Copy a block that works and adapt it to your tables, rather than starting from an
empty chunk.

### 4. The classic traps

You met each of them in the course; they all come back in real data.

- **The confounder** (Session 6): an association created by a third variable. Accessions
  from the north flower late *and* share many alleles; in a lake survey, the fish of the
  deep station are larger *and* older.
- **The batch**: samples processed on different days, by different people, in different
  experiments. Check whether it explains your first PC.
- **Thousands of tests** (Sessions 5-6): with 20,000 genes, 1,000 are "significant" at
  5% by chance alone.
- **Unequal sampling**: more observations does not mean more organisms. Ten times more
  records of a butterfly in 2020 than in 1980 tells you first about the number of
  observers.
- **Raw counts are not comparable**: a sample sequenced twice as deep has twice as many
  reads in every gene. Normalise first (counts per million, then a log).
- **Overfitting** (Session 7): a model judged on the data it learned from always looks
  good. Keep a test set.
- **Correlation is not causation**: the data are observations; say what an experiment
  would need to show.

### A. How fast does a plant answer a bacterium? {#project-a}

**The biology.** Plants have no immune cells: every cell defends itself. A first layer of
immunity recognises general bacterial molecules. Bacteria inject **effector** proteins to
suppress it, and plants evolved **resistance (R) genes** whose products recognise some of
these effectors and trigger a stronger, faster response, often with cell death at the
infection site. The strain *Pseudomonas syringae* pv. *tomato* **DC3000** is virulent on
*Arabidopsis*: no effector is recognised. The same strain carrying the effector
**AvrRpt2** (recognised by the R protein RPS2) or **AvrRpm1** (recognised by RPM1) is
avirulent: the plant wins. Several hormones carry the signal: salicylic acid (SA, needs
the gene *SID2*), and the regulators *PAD4*, *EIN2* (ethylene) and *DDE2* (jasmonate).

**The data.** Leaves of six genotypes were infiltrated with water (mock) or one of the
three strains and harvested from 1 to 24 hours later, in three independent trials (Mine
et al. 2018).

| File | One row is | Columns |
|---|---|---|
| `pseudomonas_samples.csv` | one sample (366) | `sample`, `genotype` (Col-0 = wild type, `sid2`, `pad4`, `pad4 sid2`, `dde2 ein2 pad4 sid2`, `rpm1 rps2`), `treatment` (`mock`, `DC3000`, `AvrRpt2`, `AvrRpm1`), `time_h`, `trial`, `library_size` |
| `pseudomonas_counts.csv.gz` | one gene (18,422) | `gene_id`, then one column per sample: the number of reads |
| `arabidopsis_genes.csv` | one gene | `gene_id`, `symbol`, position, `description` (as in Session 6) |

Not every genotype was tested with every strain at every time: count the samples per
combination first.

**Starting question.** Does wild-type *Arabidopsis* (Col-0) respond faster to a bacterium
it recognises (AvrRpt2, AvrRpm1) than to one it does not (DC3000)? How many genes change,
and when?

**To go further.**

- Which hormone pathway does each mutant lose? Compare the response of the mutants to
  AvrRpt2 with Col-0, and follow known marker genes (look for *PR1* in the gene table).
- In `rpm1 rps2`, the plant cannot recognise the effectors any more. Does AvrRpt2 then
  look like DC3000?
- Do the response genes fall into a few groups with the same time course (early,
  transient, late)? What are these genes?
- Is the **trial** (the day of the experiment) visible in the data?

**Tools.** Normalisation (counts per million, log); PCA (Session 5); for each time point,
the difference with mock, or one `lm()` per gene (Session 6) with a correction for
thousands of tests; heatmap and clustering (Session 5).

**Watch out.** Compare each infected sample with the **mock of the same time**: the
leaves change during the day too. A `trial` is a batch. With 18,000 genes, choose your
threshold before you look.

### B. What controls the expression of a gene in nature? {#project-b}

**The biology.** In Session 6 you found the genes behind flowering time and leaf sodium
by comparing 1,003 wild accessions. Here, the "trait" is the **expression of a gene**:
the leaves of 649 of the same accessions were sequenced (RNA-seq) before flowering, all
grown in the same chamber (Kawakatsu et al. 2016). An expression level can be controlled
by a variant **next to the gene** (in *cis*) or by a variant in **another gene** that
regulates it (in *trans*). A GWAS whose trait is an expression level is called an
**eQTL** scan.

**The data.**

| File | One row is | Columns |
|---|---|---|
| `accessions.csv` | one accession (649) | as in Session 6 (`accession_id`, `name`, `country`, `latitude`, `longitude`, `genetic_group`, `flowering_10C`, `flowering_16C`, `leaf_sodium`), plus the climate of its place of origin (WorldClim, 1970-2000): `annual_mean_temperature`, `min_temperature_coldest_month`, `annual_precipitation` and five more |
| `accession_expression.csv.gz` | one gene (20,832) | `gene_id`, then one column per accession (`acc_` + `accession_id`, in the order of `accessions.csv`): normalised read counts |
| `accession_genotypes.csv.gz` | one SNP (22,406) | as in Session 6: `snp_id`, `chromosome`, `position`, then 0/1 per accession, same order |
| `arabidopsis_genes.csv` | one gene | as in Session 6 |

**Starting question.** The gene *FLC* blocks flowering. Is its expression in the leaves
associated with flowering time? And which regions of the genome control the expression
of *FLC*: the region of *FLC* itself, or other regions?

**To go further.**

- Do the accessions group by their expression (PCA) the way they group by their DNA
  (genetic groups)?
- Which genes are expressed differently in accessions from cold and warm places? Is it
  the climate, or the ancestry that goes with it?
- Choose other genes (another flowering gene, a gene of a pathway you like) and run their
  eQTL scan: *cis*, *trans* or nothing?
- Can the expression of a few genes **predict** flowering time better than *FLC* alone
  (Session 7, with a test set)?

**Tools.** Log of the expression; `lm()` and correlation (Session 4); PCA (Session 5);
GWAS with `apply()`, Manhattan and QQ plots, PCs as covariates (Session 6); trees and
test sets (Session 7).

**Watch out.** Population structure (Session 6) is still there. A scan of 22,000 SNPs for
each of 20,000 genes would be 440 million models: choose a few genes. Climate of origin
and ancestry are confounded.

### C. When does a coral break down under heat? {#project-c}

**The biology.** A reef-building coral is a partnership: the animal hosts microscopic
algae (Symbiodiniaceae) inside its cells, which give it most of its food by
photosynthesis. When the sea gets too warm, the partnership breaks down: the coral loses
its algae and turns white (**bleaching**), and may starve. Corals of the northern Red Sea
(Gulf of Aqaba) are unusually heat-tolerant. Savary et al. (2021) exposed fragments of
five colonies (**genotypes**) of *Stylophora pistillata* to 27 °C (normal), 29.5, 32 and
34.5 °C, in a **short** experiment (a few hours, as in a heatwave) and a **long** one
(about a week), and sequenced both partners at the end of the heat and after recovery.

**The data.**

| File | One row is | Columns |
|---|---|---|
| `coral_samples.csv` | one sample (80) | `sample`, `experiment` (`short`, `long`), `time` (`end_of_heat`, `recovery`), `temperature`, `genotype`, `coral_reads`, `algae_reads`, `algae_share` |
| `coral_counts.csv.gz` | one coral gene (19,722) | `gene_id`, then one column per sample: reads |
| `algae_counts.csv.gz` | one algal gene (32,605) | the same, for the algae |
| `coral_genes.csv.gz`, `algae_genes.csv.gz` | one gene | `gene_id`, the best match in protein databases (`best_hit_description`, `best_hit_species`, `identity_percent`), `go_terms` |

**Starting question.** At which temperature does the coral switch on a large stress
response, and does it recover afterwards? Does the answer differ between the short and
the long experiment?

**To go further.**

- `algae_share` is the share of reads that come from the algae. Can it measure bleaching?
  When does it drop?
- Do the algae respond to heat like their host, at the same temperature?
- What matters more for expression: the genotype of the colony or the temperature (share
  of variation, Session 4)?
- Which kinds of genes change (look at the descriptions: heat shock proteins,
  antioxidants ...)? Are they the same in the short and the long experiment?

**Tools.** Normalisation; PCA (Session 5); `lm()` with several factors and share of
variation (Session 4); one model per gene with a correction (Sessions 5-6).

**Watch out.** Five genotypes per group: very few. Genotype is a block, as `trial` in
project A. Gene descriptions come from the best match in other species, often far away
(mouse, human): a name is a hint, not a proof.

### D. What shapes the communities of ocean bacteria? {#project-d}

**The biology.** One litre of sea water holds about a billion bacteria and archaea; they
make half of the photosynthesis on Earth and recycle most nutrients. Between 2009 and
2013, the schooner *Tara* sampled the oceans of the world at three depths: the surface
(SRF), the deep chlorophyll maximum (DCM, where algae are most abundant, 20-200 m) and
the dark mesopelagic zone (MES, 200-1,000 m). Sunagawa et al. (2015) sequenced the DNA of
everything filtered from these waters; here you receive the 16S rRNA reads, grouped into
taxa (OTUs), as in the soybean microbiome of Session 4.

**The data.**

| File | One row is | Columns |
|---|---|---|
| `tara_samples.csv` | one sample (139) | `sample`, `station`, `date`, `latitude`, `longitude`, `layer` (`SRF`, `DCM`, `MES`, `MIX`), `oxygen_minimum_zone`, `size_fraction`, `biome`, `ocean`, `depth_m`, `temperature`, `salinity`, `oxygen`, `nitrate`, `phosphate`, `silicate`, `light_par`, `bacteria_per_ml` |
| `tara_otus.csv.gz` | one OTU (10,311) | `otu_id`, taxonomy (`domain` to `genus`), then one column per sample: reads |
| `tara_functions.csv.gz` | one metabolic pathway (515 KEGG modules) | `module`, then one column per sample: abundance of the genes of that pathway |

**Starting question.** Which factor best explains the composition of the bacterial
community: depth (layer), temperature, or the ocean where it was sampled?

**To go further.**

- Is diversity (Shannon, Session 4) higher in warm or in cold water, at the surface or in
  the dark?
- Which taxa follow temperature? Look at the Cyanobacteria (*Prochlorococcus*,
  *Synechococcus*): what do they need?
- Do the **functions** (KEGG modules) vary between samples as much as the **taxa**? If
  not, what could that mean?
- What is special about the oxygen minimum zones?

**Tools.** Relative abundances, Shannon diversity and `lm()` (Session 4); PCA of the
communities (Session 5, on transformed relative abundances); maps (Sessions 4, 6).

**Watch out.** Depth, temperature, light and oxygen go together: separating them needs a
model with several variables. Two size fractions (filters) exist: do not compare them by
accident. Reads per sample differ: work with relative abundances.

### E. What does the shape of a bird tell about its life? {#project-e}

**The biology.** A bird's body is a toolkit. The beak is shaped by what it eats, the wing
by how far it flies, the legs by where it moves. AVONET (Tobias et al. 2022) measured
**90,000 museum and live birds** of almost every species alive (11,009): beak, wing,
tail, legs and mass, with each species' diet, habitat, migration and geographic range.
Classic "rules" of ecology can be tested with it, such as **Bergmann's rule**: animals are
larger in colder climates.

**The data.**

| File | One row is | Columns |
|---|---|---|
| `bird_species.csv` | one species (11,009) | `species`, `family`, `order`, `n_individuals`, beak (`beak_length_culmen`, `beak_length_nares`, `beak_width`, `beak_depth`), `tarsus_length`, `wing_length`, `kipps_distance`, `secondary1`, `hand_wing_index` (long, pointed wings are high), `tail_length`, `mass`, `traits_inferred`, `habitat`, `habitat_density`, `migration` (1 sedentary, 2 partial, 3 migratory), `trophic_level`, `trophic_niche`, `primary_lifestyle`, latitudes and `range_size` |
| `bird_individuals.csv.gz` | one measured bird (90,003) | `species`, `sex`, `specimen_type`, `country`, the same measurements |

All lengths are in mm, mass in g, latitudes in degrees.

**Starting question.** Can you tell what a bird eats from the shape of its body? Which
measurements separate the diets best?

**To go further.**

- Does a decision tree or a random forest (Session 7) predict the diet on species it has
  never seen? Which diets does it confuse, and why?
- Are birds larger closer to the poles (Bergmann's rule)? Does it still hold **within**
  families?
- Do migratory birds have longer, more pointed wings (`hand_wing_index`)?
- Within a species, do males and females differ (`bird_individuals.csv.gz`)? In which
  families most?

**Tools.** Log of the measurements; PCA (Session 5); `lm()` with a covariate (Sessions 4,
6); trees, random forest, test set and confusion matrix (Session 7).

**Watch out.** Mass dominates everything: a large bird has a large beak. Think about size
before shape (ratios, or the PCA). Species are not independent: related species
resemble each other (as related accessions in Session 6); `family` can enter the model.
Some trait values were inferred from related species (`traits_inferred`).

### F. How many immune genes does a plant need? {#project-f}

**The biology.** The resistance genes of project A belong to one big family: the
**NLRs** (nucleotide-binding, leucine-rich repeat receptors), the main intracellular
immune receptors of plants. Every NLR has a central **NB-ARC** domain. Most also carry
an N-terminal domain that defines their class: **TIR** (TNLs), a coiled coil (**CNLs**)
or **RPW8** (RNLs, "helpers" that relay the signal). TNLs need the protein **EDS1** (with
PAD4, met in project A) to signal. *Arabidopsis* has about 170 NLR genes, rice 350 to 450,
bread wheat more than 2,000, and some plants almost none.

**The data.** For the reference genome (proteome) of 413 green plants in UniProt, from
algae and mosses to trees and grasses, the genes whose proteins carry each domain
(domains detected by Pfam).

| File | One row is | Columns |
|---|---|---|
| `plant_proteomes.csv` | one species | `proteome_id`, `species`, `lineage_group` (green algae, bryophytes ..., monocots, eudicots), `order`, `family`, `n_genes`, `busco_complete` (completeness of the genome annotation, %), `nlr_genes` (genes with NB-ARC), `nlr_genes_with_lrr`, `tnl_genes`, `cnl_genes`, `rnl_genes`, `eds1_family_genes`, `lineage` |
| `nlr_genes.csv.gz` | one NLR gene | `proteome_id`, `species`, `lineage_group`, `gene`, `accession`, `length`, `class` (TNL, RNL, CNL or other), `lrr`, `domains` (all Pfam domains of the protein) |
| `pfam_domains.csv` | one Pfam domain | the names of the 30 most frequent domains |
| `nlr_proteins.fasta` | one protein | the NLR proteins of eight species, for BLAST, alignments and trees |

**Starting question.** How many NLR genes do plants have? Does this number follow their
lineage, the size of their genome, or their way of life?

**To go further.**

- Which lineages have no TNL at all? How many genes of the EDS1 family (EDS1, PAD4,
  SAG101, the partners TNLs need to signal) do they keep, compared with plants that have
  TNLs? What would that suggest about how the receptors and their partners evolved?
- Find aquatic, parasitic or carnivorous plants in the table (search their names): how
  many NLRs do they keep?
- Do NLRs appear in algae, mosses, ferns? When did the TIR and RPW8 classes appear?
- Align the NB-ARC proteins of two species and build a tree (Session 3): do the TNLs and
  the CNLs form separate groups? Do the genes of one species form clusters (recent
  duplications)?

**Tools.** Tables, `lm()` with a covariate (Session 4); `blastp`, `mafft`, `FastTree` and
a tree in R (Session 3); a model per lineage (Session 6 logic of confounders).

**Watch out.** A badly annotated genome "has" fewer genes of every kind: check
`busco_complete`. Larger gene sets have more of everything: compare shares, or put
`n_genes` in the model. Species are not independent: lineage is a confounder. A domain
not found by Pfam is not proof that the gene is absent.

### G. Will species move with the climate? {#project-g}

**The biology.** As the climate warms, many species are expected to shift their range
towards the poles and up the mountains. Citizen scientists and museums have recorded
millions of observations, gathered by **GBIF** (Global Biodiversity Information
Facility). Combined with climate maps (WorldClim), they describe the **climatic niche**
of a species: the climates where it lives. A model of presence from climate can then be
projected onto the climate expected in 2061-2080.

**The data.** Observations of eight species in Europe, two periods (1970-1999 and
2000-2024), on a grid of cells of 10 arc-minutes (about 18 × 12 km in Europe): four
warm-loving species expanding northwards (praying mantis *Mantis religiosa*, scarlet
dragonfly *Crocothemis erythraea*, wasp spider *Argiope bruennichi*, scarce swallowtail
*Iphiclides podalirius*), two cold-adapted butterflies (Apollo *Parnassius apollo*,
dewy ringlet *Erebia pandrose*) and two trees (beech *Fagus sylvatica*, Norway spruce
*Picea abies*).

| File | One row is | Columns |
|---|---|---|
| `species.csv` | one species | `species`, `common_name`, `group`, and the number of GBIF records per period |
| `occurrences.csv` | one species × period × cell where it was recorded | `species`, `period`, `longitude`, `latitude` (centre of the cell), `n_records` |
| `europe_climate.csv.gz` | one land cell of Europe | `longitude`, `latitude`, then each climate variable now (1970-2000) and in 2061-2080 under a medium (`_ssp245`) and a high (`_ssp585`) emission scenario |

**Starting question.** In which part of the European climate does each species live? Has
the range of the warm-loving species moved north between 1970-1999 and 2000-2024?

**To go further.**

- Train a model that predicts presence from climate (a tree or a random forest, Session
  7: presences against cells where the species was not recorded). Is it good on cells it
  has not seen?
- Project it onto the climate of 2061-2080 and map it (`geom_tile()`, Session 4): which
  species gain, which lose? Does the scenario matter?
- Are the cold-adapted species retreating up the mountains or north?
- Is Norway spruce really living where its "niche" says (think of forestry)?

**Tools.** Tables and maps with `geom_tile()` (Session 4); `lm()`; trees, random forest,
test set (Session 7).

**Watch out.** **Sampling effort**: there are many more records after 2000, so a species
seems to "appear" everywhere. Compare with the number of records, or use the edge of the
range (the 95th percentile of latitude) rather than the number of cells. Not recorded
does not mean absent. To keep the files small, at most 1,500 records were taken per
species, period and country. A species is recorded in a few thousand of the 38,004 cells:
a model may simply answer "absent" everywhere and still be right 86 to 98% of the time (the
accuracy trap of Session 7); one solution is to take, at random, as many cells without
record as cells with a presence. Planted trees live outside their natural niche.

::: {.callout-tip collapse="true"}
## Help for project G: from the past to the future

**Three periods, not two.** The climate called `_now` is the **average of 1970-2000**: it
matches your old observations (1970-1999), not today. There is no climate map for
2000-2024, when Europe was already warmer. The future (`_ssp245`, `_ssp585`) is
2061-2080. Say which period each figure is about.

**What links the periods: the climatic niche.** The whole project assumes that a species
keeps the same niche and moves to follow it. Your story can go: has it already moved
(past to present), and where will its climate be (present to future)?

**Past to present: has the range moved?**

- The **northern edge** of a range is the latitude below which most of the occupied cells
  lie, for example 95% of them (the 95th percentile of latitude). Why not the
  northernmost cell? One lost individual or one typing error would move it.
- More records after 2000 means more cells, everywhere. Before concluding, check that the
  edge still moves when you give both periods the same number of cells (draw the recent
  cells at random).
- The 1970-2000 climate can serve as a fixed reference map: in the recent period, does
  the species live in cells that used to be too cold for it?

**A model of presence (Session 7).**

- One row per cell: present, or not recorded (not "absent").
- Balance the two classes before training. What does a model that always says "absent"
  score?
- Keep test cells aside; the model never sees them.
- Use the climate variables only, not longitude and latitude: with the coordinates the
  tree learns *where* the species is, not *which climate* it likes.
- Which observations do you train on? The old ones match the `_now` climate in time; the
  recent ones are more numerous. Choose, and say why.
- Judge it on the test cells: confusion matrix, accuracy (what does chance give with
  balanced classes?), recall of the presences. Read the tree: is its first split
  biologically sensible?

**Present to future: projecting the model.**

- The tree knows the climate variables by their `_now` names. To give it the climate of
  2061-2080, make a table where the `_ssp245` (or `_ssp585`) columns carry the `_now`
  names. Otherwise `predict()` cannot find them, or uses the present climate without
  telling you.
- Compare, for now, the medium and the high scenario: how many cells are suitable, and
  where (their median latitude)? Maps side by side: `geom_tile()`, one facet per period.
- Does the movement you measured between 1970 and 2024 go the same way as the one the
  model predicts for 2070?

**Limits worth a slide.** A suitable climate is not a presence (dispersal, habitat, host
plants, history); cells "not recorded" were used as absences; sampling effort changed a
lot; the niche may not stay the same; there is no climate map of 2000-2024.
:::
