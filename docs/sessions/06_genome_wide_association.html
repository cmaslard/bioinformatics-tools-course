6.2 Theory (≈45 min)

6.2.1 1. The question: natural variation

Individuals of one species differ, and part of these differences is written in their DNA. A GWAS asks: at which positions of the genome do the individuals with a high value of a trait tend to carry a different letter than those with a low value?

Arabidopsis thaliana is ideal for this. It fertilises itself, so a wild line, an accession, is almost completely homozygous: its seeds are genetically identical to the parent plant. An accession can be sequenced once and grown as many times as needed, in as many conditions as needed. The 1001 Genomes project (2016) sequenced 1,135 accessions and found about 10 million positions where they differ.

6.2.2 2. The data: SNPs coded 0 and 1

A SNP (single nucleotide polymorphism) is one position of the genome where some accessions carry one letter and others another, for example A or G. Today’s genotype table keeps more than 20,000 SNPs, about one every 5,000 bases, coded:

  • 0: the accession has the same letter as the reference genome (the lab accession Col-0);
  • 1: it has the other letter.

0 does not mean “normal” and 1 does not mean “mutant”: 0 only means “like Col-0”. Each accession is homozygous, so there are only two values (in humans, who carry two different copies, the coding is 0, 1 or 2).

Only SNPs whose rarer letter is carried by at least 5% of the accessions were kept (the minor allele frequency, MAF ≥ 5%): a letter carried by 3 plants out of 1,000 would be tested on 3 plants only, and one odd plant would be enough to make it look important.

The table has one row per SNP, as in the VCF format used in genomics, and one column per accession. In R, you turn it into a matrix of 0 and 1, as you did with the metabolites in Session 5.

6.2.3 3. One linear model per SNP

For one SNP, the question is the one of Session 4: does the flowering time differ between the plants with allele 0 and those with allele 1?

lm(flowering ~ allele)

The Estimate of allele is the difference between the two means, in days; its p-value says whether the difference is larger than chance. A GWAS repeats this model for every SNP and keeps the p-values: thousands of models, each a fraction of a second.

Many tests. At the 5% level, 1 test in 20 is “significant” by chance alone: with thousands of SNPs, that makes hundreds of false positives, even if no SNP did anything. GWAS use the Bonferroni threshold: 5% divided by the number of tests. Only SNPs below it are called significant. It is stricter than the FDR correction of Session 5 (it controls the risk of even one false positive in the whole genome), and it is the usual choice in GWAS.

The Manhattan plot shows all the tests at once: the position of each SNP along the five chromosomes on the x axis, −log10(p) on the y axis (p = 0.01 becomes 2, 10⁻⁶ becomes 6: high means strong evidence), and a line at the Bonferroni threshold. A real association looks like a tower: not one point, but several neighbouring SNPs raised together, because neighbouring SNPs are inherited together (linkage disequilibrium).

6.2.4 4. Can the scan be trusted? The QQ plot

If no SNP had any effect, the p-values would be spread evenly between 0 and 1. The QQ plot compares the observed p-values (sorted) with these expected ones, both on the −log10 scale:

  • points on the diagonal, with a few rising at the top right only: a healthy scan, with a few real associations;
  • the whole curve above the diagonal, almost from the start: too many small p-values everywhere, a sign of a hidden problem.

The inflation factor λ sums up the QQ plot in one number: λ = 1 means no inflation; above about 1.1, be suspicious. It is computed from the median SNP, so a few real peaks do not change it.

6.2.5 5. The confounder: population structure

Plants from the same region share ancestry: over most of the genome, two Swedish accessions are more alike than a Swedish and a Spanish one. Thousands of letters are therefore frequent in Sweden and rare elsewhere, for purely historical reasons. If the trait also differs between regions (say, if the plants of one region flower late), then every SNP with a letter typical of that region looks associated with late flowering. The SNP did nothing: it only tells where the plant comes from. Ancestry is a confounder, like a place on the bench could have been one in the soybean experiment of Session 4, if the plants had not been randomised. In nature, nothing is randomised.

The fix. The PCA of Session 5, run on the genotype matrix (accessions in rows, SNPs in columns), finds the main axes of genetic differences between accessions: they follow ancestry, and geography. Adding the first principal components to every model,

lm(flowering ~ allele + pcs)

changes the question to: among accessions of similar ancestry, do those with allele 1 flower later? This is what adding heat next to water did in Session 4: an effect “adjusted for” another variable. Professionals go one step further with mixed models and a kinship matrix (the genetic similarity of every pair of plants); we stay with the PCs, which you already know.

6.2.6 6. From a peak to a gene

The best SNP of a tower is a marker, not necessarily the cause: it travels with the real cause, which may be another SNP, a deletion, or anything nearby. In Arabidopsis, this travelling together usually fades within about 10,000 bases, so you list the genes within ±20 kb of the best SNP, read what they do, and choose the most plausible one. Databases help: TAIR for Arabidopsis genes, UniProt for proteins (Session 3). A GWAS gives candidates; only experiments (mutants, swapping alleles between plants) prove that a gene causes the difference.

6.2.7 7. Worked example: the 1001 Genomes plants

  • Flowering time (1001 Genomes Consortium, 2016): each accession was grown in growth chambers at a constant 10 °C or 16 °C, 16 hours of light, without any winter, 4 plants per accession. The trait is the number of days from sowing to the first open flower (the mean of the 4 plants).
  • Leaf sodium (Baxter et al., 2010): plants of about 150 of these accessions were grown in the same soil, and the sodium in their leaves measured by mass spectrometry. Too much sodium is toxic for plants: it is the main problem of salty soils.
  • Genetic groups: the 1001 Genomes project grouped the accessions by ancestry (north_sweden, spain, relict, …); accessions with mixed ancestry are admixed. You will use these groups only to look at the results, never in the GWAS itself.

6.3 Your R toolbox

New tools today, on top of the Session 4 and Session 5 toolboxes (still valid: see the Session 4 R toolbox).

  • A function and apply()
  • Matrices: rows, columns, t()
  • Manhattan plot
  • QQ plot and λ
  • A map

A function packs a few lines of code under a name, so that you can run them again on other data:

snp_p_value <- function(allele) {
  fit <- lm(flowering ~ allele)
  summary(fit)$coefficients["allele", "Pr(>|t|)"]
}
  • allele is the argument: the data you give the function each time you call it;
  • the last line is what the function returns: here the p-value of the row allele in the table of coefficients of summary(fit);
  • flowering is not an argument: the function finds it in your session, so create it first.

apply() runs a function on every row (or column) of a matrix:

apply(genotype_matrix, 1, snp_p_value)   # 1 = each row, 2 = each column

It returns one value per row: here one p-value per SNP, in the order of the rows.

Common mistake. object 'flowering' not found: run the line that creates flowering before apply().

## Theory (≈45 min)

### 1. The question: natural variation

Individuals of one species differ, and part of these differences is written in their
DNA. A **GWAS** asks: *at which positions of the genome do the individuals with a high
value of a trait tend to carry a different letter than those with a low value?*

***Arabidopsis thaliana*** is ideal for this. It fertilises itself, so a wild line, an
**accession**, is almost completely homozygous: its seeds are genetically identical to
the parent plant. An accession can be sequenced once and grown as many times as needed,
in as many conditions as needed. The **1001 Genomes** project (2016) sequenced 1,135
accessions and found about 10 million positions where they differ.

### 2. The data: SNPs coded 0 and 1

A **SNP** (single nucleotide polymorphism) is one position of the genome where some
accessions carry one letter and others another, for example `A` or `G`. Today's genotype
table keeps more than 20,000 SNPs, about one every 5,000 bases, coded:

- **0**: the accession has the same letter as the reference genome (the lab accession
  Col-0);
- **1**: it has the other letter.

0 does not mean "normal" and 1 does not mean "mutant": 0 only means "like Col-0". Each
accession is homozygous, so there are only two values (in humans, who carry two
different copies, the coding is 0, 1 or 2).

Only SNPs whose rarer letter is carried by at least 5% of the accessions were kept (the
**minor allele frequency**, MAF ≥ 5%): a letter carried by 3 plants out of 1,000 would be
tested on 3 plants only, and one odd plant would be enough to make it look important.

The table has one row per SNP, as in the VCF format used in genomics, and one column per
accession. In R, you turn it into a **matrix** of 0 and 1, as you did with the
metabolites in Session 5.

### 3. One linear model per SNP

For one SNP, the question is the one of Session 4: does the flowering time differ between
the plants with allele 0 and those with allele 1?

```r
lm(flowering ~ allele)
```

The `Estimate` of `allele` is the difference between the two means, in days; its p-value
says whether the difference is larger than chance. A GWAS repeats this model for every
SNP and keeps the p-values: thousands of models, each a fraction of a second.

**Many tests.** At the 5% level, 1 test in 20 is "significant" by chance alone: with
thousands of SNPs, that makes hundreds of false positives, even if no SNP did anything. GWAS use the
**Bonferroni** threshold: 5% divided by the number of tests. Only SNPs below it are
called significant. It is stricter than the FDR correction of Session 5 (it controls the
risk of even one false positive in the whole genome), and it is the usual choice in
GWAS.

**The Manhattan plot** shows all the tests at once: the position of each SNP along the
five chromosomes on the x axis, −log10(p) on the y axis (p = 0.01 becomes 2, 10⁻⁶
becomes 6: high means strong evidence), and a line at the Bonferroni threshold. A real
association looks like a **tower**: not one point, but several neighbouring SNPs raised
together, because neighbouring SNPs are inherited together (**linkage
disequilibrium**).

### 4. Can the scan be trusted? The QQ plot

If no SNP had any effect, the p-values would be spread evenly between 0 and 1. The **QQ
plot** compares the observed p-values (sorted) with these expected ones, both on the
−log10 scale:

- points on the diagonal, with a few rising at the top right only: a healthy scan, with
  a few real associations;
- the whole curve above the diagonal, almost from the start: too many small p-values
  **everywhere**, a sign of a hidden problem.

The **inflation factor λ** sums up the QQ plot in one number: λ = 1 means no inflation;
above about 1.1, be suspicious. It is computed from the median SNP, so a few real peaks
do not change it.

### 5. The confounder: population structure

Plants from the same region share ancestry: over most of the genome, two Swedish
accessions are more alike than a Swedish and a Spanish one. Thousands of letters are
therefore frequent in Sweden and rare elsewhere, for purely historical reasons. If the
trait **also** differs between regions (say, if the plants of one region flower late),
then every SNP with a letter typical of that region looks associated with late
flowering. The SNP did nothing: it
only tells where the plant comes from. Ancestry is a **confounder**, like a place on the
bench could have been one in the soybean experiment of Session 4, if the plants had not
been randomised. In nature, nothing is randomised.

**The fix.** The PCA of Session 5, run on the genotype matrix (accessions in rows, SNPs
in columns), finds the main axes of genetic differences between accessions: they follow
ancestry, and geography. Adding the first principal components to every model,

```r
lm(flowering ~ allele + pcs)
```

changes the question to: *among accessions of similar ancestry, do those with allele 1
flower later?* This is what adding `heat` next to `water` did in Session 4: an effect
"adjusted for" another variable. Professionals go one step further with **mixed models**
and a kinship matrix (the genetic similarity of every pair of plants); we stay with the
PCs, which you already know.

### 6. From a peak to a gene

The best SNP of a tower is a **marker**, not necessarily the cause: it travels with the
real cause, which may be another SNP, a deletion, or anything nearby. In
*Arabidopsis*, this travelling together usually fades within about 10,000 bases, so you
list the genes within ±20 kb of the best SNP, read what they do, and choose the most
plausible one. Databases help: [TAIR](https://www.arabidopsis.org) for *Arabidopsis*
genes, [UniProt](https://www.uniprot.org) for proteins (Session 3). A GWAS gives
**candidates**; only experiments (mutants, swapping alleles between plants) prove that a
gene causes the difference.

## A function and apply()

A **function** packs a few lines of code under a name, so that you can run them again
on other data:

```r
snp_p_value <- function(allele) {
  fit <- lm(flowering ~ allele)
  summary(fit)$coefficients["allele", "Pr(>|t|)"]
}
```

- `allele` is the **argument**: the data you give the function each time you call it;
- the last line is what the function returns: here the p-value of the row `allele` in
  the table of coefficients of `summary(fit)`;
- `flowering` is not an argument: the function finds it in your session, so create it
  first.

`apply()` runs a function on every row (or column) of a matrix:

```r
apply(genotype_matrix, 1, snp_p_value)   # 1 = each row, 2 = each column
```

It returns one value per row: here one p-value per SNP, in the order of the rows.

**Common mistake.** `object 'flowering' not found`: run the line that creates
`flowering` before `apply()`.
