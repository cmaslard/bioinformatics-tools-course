7.3 Theory 1: what is artificial intelligence?

7.3.1 Chihuahua or muffin?

In 2016, the artist Karen Zack posted grids of photos where chihuahuas and blueberry muffins look almost the same: two dark eyes and a nose, or three blueberries. You see the difference in a fraction of a second. Now try to write the rule: “if there are three dark round spots in a triangle, then…”. It does not work: the dog can turn its head, the muffin can have four blueberries, the light changes. Computer-vision services were tested on these grids (Chihuahua or muffin? My search for the best computer vision API, 2017): they mostly succeed, and nobody wrote their rules.

This is the whole idea of machine learning: when we cannot write the rules, we give the computer examples with the right answer, and it finds the rules itself.

7.3.2 AI, machine learning, deep learning

Term What it means Example in biology
Artificial intelligence (AI) Any program that does a task we would call intelligent if a person did it. Includes rules written by people. An identification key coded as a program.
Machine learning (ML) AI that learns its rules from examples instead of being programmed with them. A decision tree that predicts the species from 4 measures (today, block B).
Deep learning (DL) Machine learning with neural networks of many layers, which also learn what to look at (the features) from raw data: pixels, sounds, sequences. A network that finds toads in camera-trap photos (block F); AlphaFold.

Each is inside the previous one: all deep learning is machine learning, all machine learning is AI, not the other way round. Generative AI (chatbots such as ChatGPT, Claude, Le Chat; image generators) is deep learning trained to produce text or images rather than to classify them.

7.3.3 The families of learning

  • Supervised learning: examples come with the answer (the label): photo → “toad”. The model learns to predict the label. Today.
  • Unsupervised learning: no label; the model finds structure by itself. The PCA and the clustering of Session 5.
  • Self-supervised learning: the model makes its own labels from the data (hide a word and guess it, hide a piece of a protein sequence and guess it). How chatbots and protein language models are trained.

And the main tasks, all present in the AnnoTrain projects of the instructor:

Detection Where are the objects? A box around each Each animal in a camera-trap photo

7.3.4 The vocabulary of supervised learning

  • A dataset is a table (or a set of photos), one row per observation (a penguin, a photo).
  • The features are what the model may look at (the measures, the pixels); the label is what it must predict (the species).
  • The model is the recipe that turns features into a prediction; its parameters (for a network: its weights) are the numbers it learns. The settings chosen by people before training (the size of a tree, the number of neurons) are hyperparameters.
  • Training adjusts the parameters so that the predictions match the labels of the training set. A validation set is used to choose hyperparameters and when to stop; a test set, never touched before the end, tells how good the model is.
  • Inference (or prediction) is using the trained model on new data.

The golden rule, which you already met with the cross-validation of Session 5: a model is only judged on data it has never seen.

7.3.5 Is the model good? The confusion matrix

Rows: the truth. Columns: what the model said. A camera trap, 100 photos, 30 with an animal:

predicted: animal predicted: empty
true: animal 24 (true positives) 6 (false negatives: missed)
true: empty 4 (false positives: invented) 66 (true negatives)
  • Accuracy = correct / all = (24 + 66) / 100 = 0.90.
  • Recall of “animal” = found / all true animals = 24 / 30 = 0.80. Did we miss animals?
  • Precision of “animal” = right / all called animal = 24 / 28 = 0.86. When it says animal, can we believe it?
  • The F1 score combines both: 2 × precision × recall / (precision + recall).

The accuracy trap. A model that answers “empty” for every photo, without looking, has an accuracy of 70 / 100 = 0.70 here, and a recall of 0 for animals. In real camera-trap projects most photos are empty, and most annotated photos belong to a few common species: always look at the recall of each class, especially the rare ones.

7.4 Theory 2: learning or memorising?

7.4.1 A decision tree

A decision tree asks yes/no questions on the features, one after the other. For three trees of a park, from their leaves: “leaf longer than 12 cm?” → chestnut; otherwise “margin toothed?” → beech if no, hornbeam if yes. To build it, the computer tries every feature and every threshold, and keeps the question that separates the labels best; then it does the same in each half, and so on. It is a dichotomous identification key, but learned from examples. It is easy to read, which is its great strength.

7.4.2 Overfitting

A tree allowed to grow without limit ends with one leaf per training example: it is then 100% right on the training data, by heart. This is overfitting: the model learns the noise and the peculiarities of the training examples instead of a rule that generalises. The signature: very good on the training set, much worse on the test set. The opposite, underfitting, is a model too simple to capture the rule (a tree with one question for three species). You will see it with shuffled labels (as in Session 5): the tree reaches 100% on training labels that mean nothing.

7.4.3 A random forest

One tree is unstable: change a few training examples and it changes. A random forest (Breiman, 2001) grows hundreds of trees, each on a bootstrap sample (the training examples drawn again at random, with replacement) and with a random subset of the features, then lets them vote. Each tree is wrong sometimes, but not on the same examples: the errors cancel out in the majority. It is the same idea as asking several people to annotate the same photo. Random forests are still among the best models for tables of measurements, and the classical pixel classifier of ilastik is one.

7.4.4 A neuron is a linear model

An artificial neuron computes a weighted sum of its inputs plus a constant, then passes it through an activation function:

\[\text{output} = f(w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + w_3 x_3 + b)\]

The part inside \(f\) is exactly the lm() of Session 4: weights (slopes) and an intercept. The activation \(f\) bends it: ReLU keeps positive values and sets negative ones to 0; the sigmoid squeezes the result between 0 and 1, like a probability. A layer is many neurons side by side; a network stacks layers, the outputs of one being the inputs of the next. With enough neurons, a network can draw any boundary between classes.

Training a network means finding the weights that make the loss (the error between predictions and labels) as small as possible. The algorithm, gradient descent, is a walker in the fog going down a hill: at each step it measures the slope of the loss for each weight and moves the weights a little downhill. One pass over all the training examples is an epoch; a network trains for tens of epochs.

7.5 Theory 3: deep learning for images

7.5.1 A photo is a table of numbers

A grey photo is a matrix: one number per pixel, from 0 (black) to 255 (white). A colour photo is three matrices (red, green, blue). A camera-trap photo of 2,048 × 1,494 pixels is 9 million numbers. A tree asking “is pixel 15,200 brighter than 120?” is useless: the animal can be anywhere, at any size, in any light. What matters are shapes, made by many pixels together.

7.5.2 Before deep learning: features designed by people

Until about 2012, image analysis started with features designed by people: brightness, colour, edges, textures, computed by hand-written formulas, then given to a classifier such as a random forest. It works on simple, well-controlled images (the instructor’s 2025 lecture on root images shows this with ilastik), and fails on the variety of the real world.

7.5.3 Convolution: filters that slide over the photo

A filter (or kernel) is a small grid of numbers, for example 3 × 3. It slides over the photo; at each position, it multiplies the 9 pixels under it by its 9 numbers and adds them up. This is a convolution. With −1 on the left and +1 on the right, the result is large where the photo changes from dark to light: the filter detects vertical edges. You will compute it yourself in block E. Then come ReLU (keep the positive answers) and pooling (keep the largest value of each 2 × 2 square, which halves the size of the image and keeps what was found).

A convolutional neural network (CNN) is a stack of such layers. The key: the numbers of the filters are weights, learned during training. The first layers learn edges and colours, the next ones combine them into textures and corners, the deeper ones into parts (an eye, a leg, a leaf margin) and whole objects. Nobody writes the features any more: they are learned from annotated examples. See them for yourself in the Feature Visualization article.

7.5.4 Why it took off around 2012

Three things came together: data (ImageNet, 14 million annotated photos, built by people annotating online), computing power (graphics cards, GPUs, which do millions of multiplications at once) and better architectures. In 2012 a CNN (AlexNet) won the ImageNet challenge by a large margin; in 2015, ResNet (He et al.) went past 150 layers. The ResNet-18 used by AnnoTrain has 18 layers and about 11 million weights.

7.5.5 Transfer learning: you do not start from zero

Training 11 million weights needs millions of photos. But the first layers of a network trained on ImageNet (edges, textures, shapes) are useful for any photo. Transfer learning (or fine-tuning) starts from such a network and retrains it on a few hundred or thousand annotated photos of your own question. This is how AnnoTrain, Teachable Machine and most biology tools work.

7.5.6 Where the labels come from

A supervised model is only as good as its labels. They come from people: experts, students, volunteers of citizen-science platforms such as Zooniverse or iNaturalist. People make mistakes and disagree, so projects ask several people per photo and keep the consensus (the majority). This “ground truth” is never perfectly true. Two classic traps: class imbalance (a few common species, many rare ones with almost no examples), and domain shift: a model trained on some cameras or some microscopes is often much worse on new ones (Beery et al., 2018).

7.5.7 AnnoTrain

AnnoTrain is the instructor’s open platform that puts the whole chain in one place: a researcher creates a project and shares a link; people annotate (open the link, answer, next photo); the platform builds the consensus, checks the answers (too-fast answers and people who disagree with reference photos do not count), trains a network (ResNet-18 for classification, detectors, U-Net and Cellpose for segmentation), and shows its training curves, its confusion matrix and its mistakes. You can use it for your Master’s project: the goal is to annotate fast, together, and to get a first model without writing code.

NoteThe whole chain

Raw photos → annotation by people → consensus (the labels) → training set / validation set → training (epochs, loss) → evaluation (confusion matrix, recall per class) → model → prediction on new photos → people check the uncertain ones → new labels → a better model.

7.6 Theory 4: AlphaFold, how a structure is predicted

7.6.1 The problem: 50 years old

A protein is a chain of amino acids that folds into a precise 3D shape, and the shape makes the function: a pocket that binds a molecule, a surface that grips DNA, a pore that lets sodium through. In 1972, Christian Anfinsen received the Nobel Prize for showing that the sequence alone contains the information of the fold. But reading it was another matter: solving one structure by experiment (X-ray crystallography, cryo-electron microscopy, NMR) takes months to years. The Protein Data Bank (PDB) holds about 200,000 solved structures, while hundreds of millions of protein sequences are known. Since 1994, a blind competition, CASP, asks teams to predict structures that have just been solved but not yet published. In 2020, AlphaFold 2 (Google DeepMind) predicted most of them with an accuracy close to the experiments. In 2024, Demis Hassabis and John Jumper shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for it (with David Baker, for protein design).

7.6.2 The idea: amino acids that touch evolve together

AlphaFold starts as you did in Session 3: it searches databases for related sequences (hundreds to thousands, from all living beings) and aligns them: a multiple sequence alignment (MSA). Two kinds of information come out of it:

  • conservation: a position that never changes is probably important (the core of the protein, the active site);
  • coevolution: if two positions always change together, they are probably in contact in 3D. A positive lysine (K) facing a negative glutamate (E) holds the fold; if a mutation turns the K into an E, the protein unfolds, unless the partner also turns into a K. Over millions of years, such pairs leave a signature of correlated changes in the alignment. You will find one in a small made-up alignment (block G6).

7.6.3 From a sequence to a structure, step by step

1. The input is only the sequence of the protein, the letters you met in the FASTA files of Session 1.

2. The search. AlphaFold searches huge databases for related sequences: UniProt, but also billions of sequences read directly from environmental DNA (soils, oceans, guts: metagenomes). It aligns them (a multiple sequence alignment, as with MAFFT in Session 3), often thousands of rows. It also looks for related proteins whose structure was solved by experiment (templates). This step takes most of the computing time of a prediction, before any neural network runs.

3. The Evoformer: two tables that talk to each other. The network keeps two tables and improves them 48 times in a row:

  • the alignment table: one row per related sequence, one column per position of the protein;
  • the pair table: one cell for every pair of positions (196 × 196 for FLC), holding the network’s current belief about how these two amino acids sit relative to each other in space. Think of it as the contact map you will draw in G4, but richer.

Each block of the Evoformer uses attention: every position looks at all the others and decides, with learned weights, which ones to listen to, as you would scan a page for the words that matter. Information flows both ways: two columns of the alignment that change together write “probably close” into the pair table (coevolution); the pair table in turn tells the network where to look in the alignment. A special step, the triangle update, checks every trio of amino acids: if A is close to B and B is close to C, then A cannot be 50 Å from C. The network was never taught geometry: it learned these rules from the examples.

4. The structure module. Each amino acid starts as a small rigid triangle (its backbone atoms N, Cα and C) floating at the same point. The module moves and turns every triangle, step by step, until their positions agree with the pair table, then places the side chains. The result: x, y, z coordinates for every atom, the numbers you will read in the PDB file in G1.

5. Recycling. The whole structure is fed back into the Evoformer, and everything runs again, three times: each pass corrects the previous one.

6. The confidence scores. During training, the network also learned to predict its own error: the pLDDT for each amino acid, the PAE for each pair. That is why AlphaFold can say “here, I do not know” (next section).

7.6.4 How it learned

Like everything today, AlphaFold is supervised learning. The examples: about 170,000 structures of the Protein Data Bank solved by experiment, each with its sequence. The label: the experimental 3D structure. The loss: for each amino acid, stand on it, look at all the other atoms, and measure how far they are from where they should be. Two more tricks:

  • fill in the blanks: some letters of the alignment are hidden, and the network must guess them, exactly like the ___ of your workbook. To guess a hidden letter, it has to understand which positions depend on which: it learns coevolution by itself;
  • learning from its own best guesses: the first version predicted about 350,000 proteins of unknown structure; the predictions it was very sure of were added to the training examples.

Training took about two weeks on 128 specialised processors (Google TPUs). A prediction then takes minutes to hours: the weights are learned once, and reused for every protein.

7.6.5 Two scores to judge a prediction

  • The pLDDT (0-100), for each amino acid: how sure AlphaFold is of the local structure. Above 90 very high, 70-90 confident, 50-70 low, below 50 very low. On the website: dark blue, light blue, yellow, orange. A very low pLDDT often means that this part of the protein is really disordered in the cell (it has no fixed shape), which is itself useful information.
  • The PAE (predicted aligned error), for each pair of amino acids: if the prediction is superposed on the true structure using amino acid A, how far off (in Å) will amino acid B be? Small inside a domain, large between two domains linked by a flexible piece: AlphaFold can be sure of the shape of each domain and unsure of how they sit together.

7.6.6 What AlphaFold 2 does not do

  • It predicts one chain alone, in one state: no DNA, no ligand, no partner protein, no movement. (AlphaFold 3, 2024, adds complexes with DNA, RNA and small molecules: try the free AlphaFold Server.)
  • It needs relatives: a protein with few known relatives has a poor alignment, and a poor prediction.
  • It is not reliable to predict the effect of a single mutation: the prediction of the mutant usually looks like the normal protein.
  • A prediction is a hypothesis, to be checked with experiments, like a GWAS peak.

7.9.9 Using AI well as a biologist

  • Use an existing tool first. Many questions already have a trained model (Pl@ntNet, BirdNET, MegaDetector, Cellpose, AlphaFold). Train your own (AnnoTrain, Teachable Machine) only when none fits.

## Theory 1: what is artificial intelligence? {#theory-1}

### Chihuahua or muffin?

In 2016, the artist Karen Zack posted grids of photos where chihuahuas and blueberry
muffins look almost the same: two dark eyes and a nose, or three blueberries. You see the
difference in a fraction of a second. Now try to **write the rule**: "if there are three
dark round spots in a triangle, then...". It does not work: the dog can turn its head, the
muffin can have four blueberries, the light changes. Computer-vision services were tested
on these grids ([Chihuahua or muffin? My search for the best computer vision
API](https://www.freecodecamp.org/news/chihuahua-or-muffin-my-search-for-the-best-computer-vision-api-cbda4d6b425d/),
2017): they mostly succeed, and nobody wrote their rules.

This is the whole idea of machine learning: **when we cannot write the rules, we give the
computer examples with the right answer, and it finds the rules itself.**

### AI, machine learning, deep learning

| Term | What it means | Example in biology |
|---|---|---|
| **Artificial intelligence** (AI) | Any program that does a task we would call intelligent if a person did it. Includes rules written by people. | An identification key coded as a program. |
| **Machine learning** (ML) | AI that **learns its rules from examples** instead of being programmed with them. | A decision tree that predicts the species from 4 measures (today, block B). |
| **Deep learning** (DL) | Machine learning with **neural networks of many layers**, which also learn *what to look at* (the features) from raw data: pixels, sounds, sequences. | A network that finds toads in camera-trap photos (block F); AlphaFold. |

Each is inside the previous one: all deep learning is machine learning, all machine
learning is AI, not the other way round. **Generative AI** (chatbots such as ChatGPT,
Claude, Le Chat; image generators) is deep learning trained to *produce* text or images
rather than to classify them.

### The families of learning

- **Supervised learning**: examples come with the answer (the **label**): photo → "toad".
  The model learns to predict the label. *Today.*
- **Unsupervised learning**: no label; the model finds structure by itself. *The PCA and
  the clustering of Session 5.*
- **Self-supervised learning**: the model makes its own labels from the data (hide a word
  and guess it, hide a piece of a protein sequence and guess it). How chatbots and protein
  language models are trained.

And the main **tasks**, all present in the AnnoTrain projects of the instructor:

| Task | Question | Output | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Classification | Which class? | One label per photo | Animal or not; which amphibian species |
| Regression | How much? | A number | Age of a fish from a scale; leaf area |
| Detection | Where are the objects? | A box around each | Each animal in a camera-trap photo |
| Segmentation | Which pixels belong to what? | A mask | Roots, shoots and seeds on a plate; each cell of a tissue |
| Tracking | Where does each object go? | A path per individual | Fish swimming in a video |
| Generation | Produce something new | Text, image, sequence | A chatbot's answer; a designed protein |

### The vocabulary of supervised learning

- A **dataset** is a table (or a set of photos), one row per **observation** (a penguin,
  a photo).
- The **features** are what the model may look at (the measures, the pixels); the
  **label** is what it must predict (the species).
- The **model** is the recipe that turns features into a prediction; its **parameters**
  (for a network: its **weights**) are the numbers it learns. The settings chosen by
  people before training (the size of a tree, the number of neurons) are
  **hyperparameters**.
- **Training** adjusts the parameters so that the predictions match the labels of the
  **training set**. A **validation set** is used to choose hyperparameters and when to
  stop; a **test set**, never touched before the end, tells how good the model is.
- **Inference** (or prediction) is using the trained model on new data.

The golden rule, which you already met with the cross-validation of Session 5: **a model is
only judged on data it has never seen.**

### Is the model good? The confusion matrix

Rows: the truth. Columns: what the model said. A camera trap, 100 photos, 30 with an
animal:

| | predicted: animal | predicted: empty |
|---|---:|---:|
| **true: animal** | 24 (true positives) | 6 (false negatives: missed) |
| **true: empty** | 4 (false positives: invented) | 66 (true negatives) |

- **Accuracy** = correct / all = (24 + 66) / 100 = 0.90.
- **Recall** of "animal" = found / all true animals = 24 / 30 = 0.80. *Did we miss
  animals?*
- **Precision** of "animal" = right / all called animal = 24 / 28 = 0.86. *When it says
  animal, can we believe it?*
- The **F1 score** combines both: 2 × precision × recall / (precision + recall).

**The accuracy trap.** A model that answers "empty" for every photo, without looking, has
an accuracy of 70 / 100 = 0.70 here, and a recall of 0 for animals. In real camera-trap
projects most photos are empty, and most annotated photos belong to a few common species:
always look at the recall of each class, especially the rare ones.

## Theory 2: learning or memorising? {#theory-2}

### A decision tree

A **decision tree** asks yes/no questions on the features, one after the other. For three
trees of a park, from their leaves: "leaf longer than 12 cm?" → chestnut; otherwise
"margin toothed?" → beech if no, hornbeam if yes. To build it, the computer tries every feature and every threshold, and keeps the
question that separates the labels best; then it does the same in each half, and so on.
It is a dichotomous identification key, but **learned from examples**. It is easy to read,
which is its great strength.

### Overfitting

A tree allowed to grow without limit ends with one leaf per training example: it is then
100% right on the training data, by heart. This is **overfitting**: the model learns the
noise and the peculiarities of the training examples instead of a rule that generalises.
The signature: very good on the training set, much worse on the test set. The opposite,
**underfitting**, is a model too simple to capture the rule (a tree with one question for
three species). You will see it with shuffled labels (as in Session 5): the tree reaches
100% on training labels that mean nothing.

### A random forest

One tree is unstable: change a few training examples and it changes. A **random forest**
(Breiman, 2001) grows hundreds of trees, each on a **bootstrap** sample (the training
examples drawn again at random, with replacement) and with a random subset of the
features, then lets them **vote**. Each tree is wrong sometimes, but not on the same
examples: the errors cancel out in the majority. It is the same idea as asking several
people to annotate the same photo. Random forests are still among the best models for
tables of measurements, and the classical pixel classifier of
[ilastik](https://www.ilastik.org) is one.

### A neuron is a linear model

An artificial **neuron** computes a weighted sum of its inputs plus a constant, then
passes it through an **activation function**:

$$\text{output} = f(w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + w_3 x_3 + b)$$

The part inside $f$ is exactly the `lm()` of Session 4: weights (slopes) and an intercept.
The activation $f$ bends it: **ReLU** keeps positive values and sets negative ones to 0; the
**sigmoid** squeezes the result between 0 and 1, like a probability. A **layer** is many
neurons side by side; a **network** stacks layers, the outputs of one being the inputs of
the next. With enough neurons, a network can draw any boundary between classes.

**Training** a network means finding the weights that make the **loss** (the error between
predictions and labels) as small as possible. The algorithm, **gradient descent**, is a
walker in the fog going down a hill: at each step it measures the slope of the loss for
each weight and moves the weights a little downhill. One pass over all the training
examples is an **epoch**; a network trains for tens of epochs.

## Theory 3: deep learning for images {#theory-3}

### A photo is a table of numbers

A grey photo is a matrix: one number per pixel, from 0 (black) to 255 (white). A colour
photo is three matrices (red, green, blue). A camera-trap photo of 2,048 × 1,494 pixels is
9 million numbers. A tree asking "is pixel 15,200 brighter than 120?" is useless: the
animal can be anywhere, at any size, in any light. What matters are **shapes**, made by
many pixels together.

### Before deep learning: features designed by people

Until about 2012, image analysis started with **features designed by people**: brightness,
colour, edges, textures, computed by hand-written formulas, then given to a classifier
such as a random forest. It works on simple, well-controlled images (the instructor's
2025 lecture on root images shows this with ilastik), and fails on the variety of the
real world.

### Convolution: filters that slide over the photo

A **filter** (or kernel) is a small grid of numbers, for example 3 × 3. It slides over the
photo; at each position, it multiplies the 9 pixels under it by its 9 numbers and adds
them up. This is a **convolution**. With −1 on the left and +1 on the right, the result is
large where the photo changes from dark to light: the filter detects vertical edges. You
will compute it yourself in block E. Then come **ReLU** (keep the positive answers) and
**pooling** (keep the largest value of each 2 × 2 square, which halves the size of the
image and keeps what was found).

A **convolutional neural network** (CNN) is a stack of such layers. The key: **the numbers
of the filters are weights, learned during training.** The first layers learn edges and
colours, the next ones combine them into textures and corners, the deeper ones into parts
(an eye, a leg, a leaf margin) and whole objects. Nobody writes the features any more:
they are learned from annotated examples. See them for yourself in the [Feature
Visualization](https://distill.pub/2017/feature-visualization/) article.

### Why it took off around 2012

Three things came together: **data** (ImageNet, 14 million annotated photos, built by
people annotating online), **computing power** (graphics cards, GPUs, which do millions of
multiplications at once) and better **architectures**. In 2012 a CNN (AlexNet) won the
ImageNet challenge by a large margin; in 2015, **ResNet** (He et al.) went past 150 layers.
The ResNet-18 used by AnnoTrain has 18 layers and about 11 million weights.

### Transfer learning: you do not start from zero

Training 11 million weights needs millions of photos. But the first layers of a network
trained on ImageNet (edges, textures, shapes) are useful for any photo. **Transfer
learning** (or fine-tuning) starts from such a network and retrains it on a few hundred or
thousand annotated photos of your own question. This is how AnnoTrain, Teachable Machine
and most biology tools work.

### Where the labels come from

A supervised model is only as good as its labels. They come from people: experts,
students, volunteers of citizen-science platforms such as
[Zooniverse](https://www.zooniverse.org) or [iNaturalist](https://www.inaturalist.org).
People make mistakes and disagree, so projects ask several people per photo and keep the
**consensus** (the majority). This "**ground truth**" is never perfectly true. Two classic
traps: **class imbalance** (a few common species, many rare ones with almost no examples),
and **domain shift**: a model trained on some cameras or some microscopes is often much
worse on new ones (Beery et al., 2018).

### AnnoTrain

[AnnoTrain](https://annotrain.cmaslard.xyz) is the instructor's open platform that puts the
whole chain in one place: a researcher creates a project and shares a link; people
annotate (open the link, answer, next photo); the platform builds the consensus, checks
the answers (too-fast answers and people who disagree with reference photos do not
count), trains a network (ResNet-18 for classification, detectors, U-Net and Cellpose for
segmentation), and shows its training curves, its confusion matrix and its mistakes. You
can use it for your Master's project: the goal is to annotate fast, together, and to get
a first model without writing code.

::: callout-note
## The whole chain

Raw photos → **annotation** by people → **consensus** (the labels) → **training set /
validation set** → **training** (epochs, loss) → **evaluation** (confusion matrix, recall
per class) → **model** → **prediction** on new photos → people check the uncertain ones →
new labels → a better model.
:::

## Theory 4: AlphaFold, how a structure is predicted {#theory-4}

### The problem: 50 years old

A protein is a chain of amino acids that folds into a precise 3D shape, and the shape makes
the function: a pocket that binds a molecule, a surface that grips DNA, a pore that lets
sodium through. In 1972, Christian Anfinsen received the Nobel Prize for showing that the
sequence alone contains the information of the fold. But reading it was another matter:
solving one structure by experiment (X-ray crystallography, cryo-electron microscopy, NMR)
takes months to years. The Protein Data Bank ([PDB](https://www.rcsb.org)) holds about
200,000 solved structures, while hundreds of millions of protein sequences are known.
Since 1994, a blind competition, [CASP](https://predictioncenter.org), asks teams to predict
structures that have just been solved but not yet published. In 2020, AlphaFold 2 (Google
DeepMind) predicted most of them with an accuracy close to the experiments. In 2024, Demis
Hassabis and John Jumper shared the [Nobel Prize in
Chemistry](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/chemistry/2024/summary/) for it (with David
Baker, for protein design).

### The idea: amino acids that touch evolve together

AlphaFold starts as you did in Session 3: it **searches databases for related sequences**
(hundreds to thousands, from all living beings) and **aligns them**: a multiple sequence
alignment (MSA). Two kinds of information come out of it:

- **conservation**: a position that never changes is probably important (the core of the
  protein, the active site);
- **coevolution**: if two positions always change *together*, they are probably in
  contact in 3D. A positive lysine (K) facing a negative glutamate (E) holds the fold; if a
  mutation turns the K into an E, the protein unfolds, unless the partner also turns into a
  K. Over millions of years, such pairs leave a signature of correlated changes in the
  alignment. You will find one in a small made-up alignment (block G6).

### From a sequence to a structure, step by step

```{mermaid}
%%| echo: false
flowchart TB
  A["Step 1: Sequence: MGRKKLEIKR... (196 amino acids)"] --> B["Step 2: Search: related sequences (alignment) and known structures"]
  B --> C["Step 3: Evoformer x 48: the alignment table and the pair table talk to each other"]
  C --> D["Step 4: Structure module: the 3D position of every atom"]
  D -- "Step 5: recycling x 3" --> C
  D --> E["Step 6: Output: structure + pLDDT + PAE"]
```

**1. The input** is only the sequence of the protein, the letters you met in the FASTA files
of Session 1.

**2. The search.** AlphaFold searches huge databases for related sequences: UniProt, but
also billions of sequences read directly from environmental DNA (soils, oceans, guts:
metagenomes). It aligns them (a multiple sequence alignment, as with MAFFT in Session 3),
often thousands of rows. It also looks for related proteins whose structure was solved
by experiment (**templates**). This step takes most of the computing time of a
prediction, before any neural network runs.

**3. The Evoformer: two tables that talk to each other.** The network keeps two tables
and improves them 48 times in a row:

- the **alignment table**: one row per related sequence, one column per position of the
  protein;
- the **pair table**: one cell for every pair of positions (196 × 196 for *FLC*), holding
  the network's current belief about how these two amino acids sit relative to each other
  in space. Think of it as the contact map you will draw in G4, but richer.

Each block of the Evoformer uses **attention**: every position looks at all the others and
decides, with learned weights, which ones to listen to, as you would scan a page for the
words that matter. Information flows both ways: two columns of the alignment that change
together write "probably close" into the pair table (coevolution); the pair table in turn
tells the network where to look in the alignment. A special step, the **triangle
update**, checks every trio of amino acids: if A is close to B and B is close to C, then A
cannot be 50 Å from C. The network was never taught geometry: it learned these rules from
the examples.

**4. The structure module.** Each amino acid starts as a small rigid triangle (its
backbone atoms N, Cα and C) floating at the same point. The module moves and turns every
triangle, step by step, until their positions agree with the pair table, then places the
side chains. The result: x, y, z coordinates for every atom, the numbers you will read in
the PDB file in G1.

**5. Recycling.** The whole structure is fed back into the Evoformer, and everything runs
again, three times: each pass corrects the previous one.

**6. The confidence scores.** During training, the network also learned to predict **its
own error**: the pLDDT for each amino acid, the PAE for each pair. That is why AlphaFold
can say "here, I do not know" (next section).

### How it learned

Like everything today, AlphaFold is **supervised learning**. The examples: about 170,000
structures of the Protein Data Bank solved by experiment, each with its sequence. The
label: the experimental 3D structure. The loss: for each amino acid, stand on it, look at
all the other atoms, and measure how far they are from where they should be. Two more
tricks:

- **fill in the blanks**: some letters of the alignment are hidden, and the network must
  guess them, exactly like the `___` of your workbook. To guess a hidden letter, it has to
  understand which positions depend on which: it learns coevolution by itself;
- **learning from its own best guesses**: the first version predicted about 350,000
  proteins of unknown structure; the predictions it was very sure of were added to the
  training examples.

Training took about two weeks on 128 specialised processors (Google TPUs). A prediction
then takes minutes to hours: the weights are learned once, and reused for every protein.

### Two scores to judge a prediction

- The **pLDDT** (0-100), for each amino acid: how sure AlphaFold is of the local structure.
  Above 90 very high, 70-90 confident, 50-70 low, below 50 very low. On the website: dark
  blue, light blue, yellow, orange. A very low pLDDT often means that this part of the
  protein is really **disordered** in the cell (it has no fixed shape), which is itself
  useful information.
- The **PAE** (predicted aligned error), for each pair of amino acids: if the prediction is
  superposed on the true structure using amino acid A, how far off (in Å) will amino acid B
  be? Small inside a domain, large between two domains linked by a flexible piece:
  AlphaFold can be sure of the shape of each domain and unsure of how they sit together.

### What AlphaFold 2 does not do

- It predicts **one chain alone**, in **one state**: no DNA, no ligand, no partner
  protein, no movement. (AlphaFold 3, 2024, adds complexes with DNA, RNA and small
  molecules: try the free [AlphaFold Server](https://alphafoldserver.com).)
- It needs **relatives**: a protein with few known relatives has a poor alignment, and a
  poor prediction.
- It is not reliable to predict the **effect of a single mutation**: the prediction of the
  mutant usually looks like the normal protein.
- A prediction is a **hypothesis**, to be checked with experiments, like a GWAS peak.

### Using AI well as a biologist

- **Use an existing tool first.** Many questions already have a trained model (Pl@ntNet,
  BirdNET, MegaDetector, Cellpose, AlphaFold). Train your own (AnnoTrain, Teachable Machine)
  only when none fits.
- **Check it on your own data.** A model trained elsewhere can fail on your cameras, your
  region, your microscope (domain shift). Annotate a sample yourself and compute the recall
  per class before trusting the counts.
- **Look at more than the accuracy**: the confusion matrix, the recall of the rare classes,
  the photos where the model is unsure.
- **Report what you used**: the tool, its version, its settings, and how you checked it.
- **Chatbots are excellent assistants and unreliable sources.** Ask them to explain an
  error message or a line of code, to suggest a plot; never trust a number, a reference
  or a fact without checking it (block G2). They write fluent text even when they invent.
- **Do not paste** unpublished data, personal data, or confidential documents into an
  online AI: you remain responsible for them.
